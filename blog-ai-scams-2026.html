AI Scams to Watch For Right Now

By The A.I. NOW crew · Detroit · November 2026 · ← Back to THE SIGNAL

Scammers have always used whatever tool works. Right now the tool is AI — and it fixes their two oldest weaknesses. Their fake messages used to have bad grammar; now they're flawless. Their impersonations used to sound like strangers; now they can sound like your own family. The good news: every one of these scams still breaks the same way — the moment you slow down and verify through a channel you chose. Here are the five big ones running right now, how each works, and the exact defense for each.

1. The voice-clone "family emergency" call

How it works: your phone rings. It's your grandchild, your son, your sister — the actual voice — crying, saying there's been an accident or an arrest, begging you to send money and not tell anyone. It's not them. A scammer lifted a few seconds of their real voice from a social video and cloned it. Sometimes a second voice joins as a "lawyer" or "officer" to add pressure. The panic is the product: they need you to pay before you think.

🛡️ The defense: (1) Set a family code word today — one word a real family member can say that a scammer can't guess. (2) Hang up and call the person back on the number saved in your phone — not the number that called you. (3) Ask something only the real person would know. A real emergency survives a two-minute callback. A scam doesn't.

2. Deepfake celebrity crypto endorsements

How it works: a video appears of a famous face — a billionaire, a TV host, a beloved actor — explaining a "new investment program" and promising to double your crypto. The face moves right. The voice sounds right. It's an AI deepfake, built without that person's involvement, and the "program" is a fake site that takes your deposit and vanishes. The famous face is there to borrow trust the scammer hasn't earned.

🛡️ The defense: nobody — famous or not — will double your money for sending them crypto first. That sentence is the whole scam, every time. Real companies don't pitch investments through random video ads, and real announcements live on official, verifiable channels. If an "opportunity" can't survive you closing the video and checking the company's real website, it was never real.

3. Fake AI trading bots & "guaranteed return" groups

How it works: an ad or a friendly stranger in a group chat promotes an "AI trading bot" that supposedly never loses. You deposit a little; a slick dashboard shows it "growing." You're encouraged to go bigger. When you try to withdraw, there are suddenly fees — pay those and there are more. The dashboard was fiction the whole time. The AI story is just a costume: guaranteed returns were impossible long before AI, and they're impossible with it.

🛡️ The defense: "Guaranteed" returns do not exist. Anyone using that word about investing is the risk. Real brokers are registered and checkable — in the US you can look firms and brokers up free through FINRA's BrokerCheck and the SEC's investor.gov. Never invest through a chat app, never let a stranger "guide" your trades, and never pay a "withdrawal fee" to unlock your own money.

4. AI phishing — emails and texts that read perfectly

How it works: the classic scam email gave itself away with broken English and weird greetings. AI fixed that. Now the "bank alert," "delivery problem" or "payroll update" message reads like it was written by the company's own staff, in your language, with your name and details scraped from the public internet. The link still leads to a fake login page built to steal your password — the writing just stopped being the warning sign.

🛡️ The defense: stop judging messages by how professional they look — judge them by where they send you. Don't use the link or button in the message. Open the company's app or type its real address yourself. Turn on two-factor authentication (an authenticator app or passkey beats text codes), because a stolen password alone should never be enough to empty an account.

5. Fake customer-service numbers and "support" scams

How it works: your account has a "problem," so you search for the company's support number — and call a fake one planted in search results or social replies. Or a pop-up freezes your screen with a number to call. The "agent" is warm, patient and professional. Then comes the ask: install a remote-access app "so I can fix it," read out a code that just texted you, or pay the "fee" with gift cards. AI makes their scripts smoother and their fake sites prettier; the ask is still the tell.

🛡️ The defense: get support numbers from the back of your card, the official app, or the company's real website — never from a pop-up or a search ad. Real support will never ask you to pay with gift cards or crypto, never needs you to install remote-access software, and never asks for the code that was just texted to you. If any of those three happens, hang up — you were never talking to support.
🚨 The three rules that beat almost all of it: (1) Urgency is the weapon — any message that needs you to act right now, in secret is lying to you. (2) Never pay a stranger with gift cards, crypto, or a wire — those payments are one-way doors, and legitimate bills never demand them. (3) Verify on a channel you chose — your saved contact, the official app, the number on your card. The scammer controls every channel they hand you. They control none of the ones you pick yourself.

If you already sent money: contact your bank immediately (speed matters), report it to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov, and tell a family member — not because you should be embarrassed, but because these scams are engineered by professionals and because the people you tell become harder targets. Staying quiet is the scammer's favorite outcome.
