💡 AI at work, explained

No doom, no hype — just what these tools actually do all day, so you can decide what's useful to you.

🤖 AI customer-service bots
Those chat boxes on store websites are mostly AI now. They answer the routine questions — hours, tracking, returns — around the clock, and hand the conversation to a human when it gets complicated. For a small shop, that means fewer missed customers at 2 AM. For customers, it means the complicated problems still need a person — and the good businesses keep one reachable.
✍️ AI marketing & copy tools
AI drafts the first version of your posts, ads, product descriptions and emails in seconds. It doesn't know your customers like you do, so the workflow that works is: AI writes the draft, you fix the voice and the facts. Owners who feed it their own real words — past posts, real prices, real stories — get copy that sounds like them instead of like an ad factory.
🧾 AI bookkeeping & admin
Software now sorts receipts, matches expenses to bank lines, drafts invoice reminders and turns meeting recordings into action lists. This is the least glamorous and most useful corner of work AI: it eats the paperwork pile. It doesn't replace your accountant or your judgment — it means the numbers are tidy enough for both to work with.
🎯 AI in hiring — a note for job hunters
Many companies let software do the first sort of applications before a person ever sees them. What actually helps: apply with a clean, simply formatted resume, use the same words the job posting uses for the skills you genuinely have, and keep everything on it true — employers verify, and real experience described well beats a decorated resume every time. A human still makes the final call at most places; your job is to get past the sorting machine honestly.
🔄 Which work is changing fastest
The pattern isn't whole jobs vanishing overnight — it's tasks changing first. First drafts of routine writing, data entry, basic scheduling, simple customer questions and stock-photo-style design are the tasks AI handles most today, in offices and shops alike. The work that stays most human: judgment calls, skilled hands-on work, relationships, and anything where being wrong is expensive. Knowing which of your tasks are "first draft" tasks is the real career skill right now.

💼 The Small Business AI Starter Pack — copy-ready prompts for real work

Hit Copy, paste into ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or any AI chat, and swap the [brackets] for your details. Always read the draft before it goes out — your name is on it, not the AI's.

Answer a customer email

Draft a reply to the customer email below for my business, [business name]. Friendly and professional, answer their actual question, and don't promise anything I didn't write in my notes. My notes: [your key points]. Their email: [PASTE THE EMAIL HERE]

Write a product description

Write a product description for [product] that costs $[price]. Facts you must use exactly: [materials, size, colors, what it does]. Short and honest — no hype words, no fake scarcity, nothing I can't prove. Give me one version for the product page and one short version for social media.

Plan a week of social posts

Plan 5 social media posts for [business name], a [type of business] in [city]. Mix: one helpful tip, one behind-the-scenes, one product, one customer question, one community post. Write the caption for each in my voice — casual, human, no hype. My products/services are: [list].

Reply to a tough review

Write a calm public reply to this review of my business. Thank them, don't get defensive, don't argue facts in public, acknowledge the specific problem they named, and invite them to contact me directly at [email/phone] so I can make it right. Keep it under 80 words. [PASTE THE REVIEW HERE]

A polite late-payment nudge

Write a polite but clear email to a client about invoice [number] for $[amount], which was due on [date] and is now [number] days past due. Friendly first reminder tone — assume it may be an oversight. Include the amount, the invoice number, and how they can pay: [payment method].

Write a job posting

Write a job posting for a [job title] at my [type of business] in [city]. Real duties: [list the actual work]. Pay range: [range]. Must-haves: [real requirements only]. Plain language, no corporate buzzwords, and include how to apply: [method].

Turn meeting notes into action items

Turn these meeting notes into a clean summary: what was decided, who owns each task, and any deadlines mentioned. If a task has no owner or no date in the notes, list it under "Unassigned — needs a decision" instead of guessing. [PASTE YOUR NOTES HERE]

Build my FAQ page

Write an FAQ section for my business website. My business: [name, what you sell/do, city]. Use only these facts: hours [hours], location [address], prices [price range], policies [returns/delivery/etc.]. Write the 8 questions customers ask most, answered in one or two plain sentences each. If a question needs a fact I didn't give you, mark it [NEEDS ANSWER] instead of inventing one.

Explain my month in numbers

Here are my business numbers for this month: [paste sales, expenses, hours — whatever you track]. Explain them back to me in plain English: what went up, what went down, anything that looks unusual, and 3 questions I should ask myself before next month. Use only the numbers I gave you — don't invent figures.

A 30-second pitch for my business

Write a 30-second spoken pitch for [business name]. We help [who] with [what problem], and what makes us different is [real difference — years in business, local, family-run, whatever is true]. Conversational, like I'd actually say it to a neighbor. No hype words, no promises I can't keep.
