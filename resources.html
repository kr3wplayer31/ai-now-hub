Resource 01 · The Basics

What an AI chatbot actually does for a small business website

You've seen the little bubble in the corner of websites: "Hi! How can I help?" Behind that bubble, on a modern site, is an AI chatbot. Forget the hype for a minute — here's what it mechanically does for a small business, job by job.

1. It answers instantly — at 2 PM and at 2 AM

Most small business websites are brochures: they sit there, and if a visitor has a question, the options are call (during hours you may not be open), email (and wait), or a contact form (and wait longer). A chatbot changes the website from a brochure into a staffed counter. A homeowner with a leaking pipe at 11 PM, a patient checking whether you take their insurance on a Sunday, a bride pricing catering from her couch — they ask, and they get an answer in seconds, written from your actual business information.

That matters because of how people actually shop now: they don't wait. A visitor who can't get an answer usually doesn't bookmark your page for Monday. They go back to the search results and ask your competitor instead.

2. It's trained on YOUR business — not the whole internet

A properly set up business chatbot isn't a general AI guessing at answers. It's trained on a specific file of facts: your services, your prices or price ranges, your hours, your service area, your booking process, your policies. Ask it something in that file and it answers from the file. Ask it something outside — a legal question for a law firm's bot, a diagnosis for a clinic's bot — and a well-built one is set up to stop, say so, and hand off to a human instead of improvising. That boundary-setting is most of the setup work, and it's why a business chatbot is a different thing from a free general-purpose AI chat window.

3. It captures the lead instead of losing the visit

Answering questions is only half the job. When a visitor is interested, the chatbot asks for the things you'd ask for: name, phone number, what they need, how urgent it is. That information lands with you — immediately — instead of evaporating when the visitor closes the tab. Paired with automatic follow-up, the visitor gets a text or email within moments saying a real person has their request. Compare that to the industry-standard experience: a form submitted into a void, answered tomorrow if the office remembers to check.

4. It does the repetitive work your staff hates

Every business has the same ten questions: hours, location, price range, do you serve my area, do you take my insurance, how do I book, what's your cancellation policy. Your staff answers them all day, every day, while trying to do their actual jobs. A chatbot absorbs that entire layer — which also means the phone calls that do come through are the ones that actually need a human.

What it does NOT do

  • It doesn't do your trade. It won't fix the roof, cut the hair, or see the patient. It books, answers, and routes.
  • It doesn't guarantee sales. It closes the gap between "someone was interested" and "someone got an answer." Closing the job is still your craft and your crew.
  • It isn't perfect. Any AI can occasionally get something wrong. Good setup — tight training, clear boundaries, testing before launch, tuning after — keeps that rare, and a good provider adjusts it when you spot something off.
  • It doesn't replace your people. It replaces the ringing phone nobody could get to and the inbox nobody had time for.

The one-sentence version

An AI chatbot turns your website from a sign that says "we exist" into an employee who says "here's the answer, and I've got your number — the owner will call you back." For most local businesses, that difference is the whole ballgame.

How much should AI cost a small business? (And why we charge 60% of agency rates)

Ask five providers what AI for your business costs and you'll get five wildly different numbers — a $30-a-month app, a freelancer's weekend quote, and an agency proposal with more zeros than your truck payment. Here's how to think about the pricing, and then — because we ask you to trust our numbers — exactly how ours work.

The three ways businesses buy this, and what each really costs

1. Do-it-yourself tools. The software itself can be cheap — sometimes tens of dollars a month. The real cost is your time: learning the tool, training it on your business, wiring it into your site, testing it, and fixing it when it says something wrong to a customer. If you enjoy that work, this is a fine route. If your hours are worth money — and an owner's usually are — the "cheap" option often isn't.

2. Freelancers. Mid-range pricing, wildly variable quality. The good ones are excellent; the risk is that your AI's entire support system is one person's phone, and if they move on, so does your maintenance.

3. Agencies. The full-service option: they design it, build it, install it, and maintain it. Also the most expensive — because an agency's price has to cover account managers, salespeople, project managers, office overhead, and profit margin stacked on top of the actual work. The work itself is real. So is the bloat.

What you're actually paying for, whoever you hire

  • Setup labor: gathering your business information, training the AI on it, setting its boundaries, and installing it on your website.
  • The platform: the AI service itself runs on paid infrastructure — somebody's monthly bill covers it, either yours directly or baked into your provider's price.
  • Maintenance: your hours, prices and services change; the AI has to change with them, and someone has to check it's answering correctly.
  • Accountability: when it says something wrong to a customer at midnight, whose problem is it? That answer is worth money, and it's the thing the cheapest options don't include.

Why we charge 60%

Our prices are set at 60% of what typical agencies charge for the same deliverables. That's not a sale or a limited-time discount — it's arithmetic, and it works because of what we don't have: no sales team on commission, no account-manager layer between you and the person doing the work, no office overhead being passed down the line. The other honest factor: we use AI in our own operation. The technology we sell does the heavy lifting in our business too — drafting, organizing, following up — so a lean operation can do work that used to take a staff. We pass that efficiency into the price instead of pocketing all of it.

Concretely: our AI Website Chatbot is $599 setup + $89/month — 60% of the roughly $1,000 setup a typical agency charges for the same install. Our packages run from there up to a full front-office bundle. Every price is printed on our Pricing page, because a price you have to book a sales call to hear is a price designed to change depending on who's asking.

Four pricing red flags — whoever you buy from

  • Guaranteed results. "We'll double your leads" is not a promise anyone can keep. AI closes response gaps; it cannot promise revenue.
  • Long lock-in contracts. If the work is good, month-to-month should be fine. A provider who needs two years of your signature to feel safe is telling you something.
  • "Starting at" with no floor explained. Starting-at pricing is legitimate — jobs differ — but you should always get the exact total in writing before work begins, and the quoted price should be the price you pay.
  • You don't own your stuff. If you leave, your website, your content and your customer data should leave with you. If a contract says otherwise, walk.

The bottom line

For a small business, AI should cost about what a modest, useful employee tool costs — because that's what it is. A fair price is one you can read before you call, understand without a translator, and cancel without a lawyer.
